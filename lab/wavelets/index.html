Lab · Lesson 6

Wavelets for engineers

Derive what a wavelet map shows for a steady tone and for a sharp knock, why every mark has the same area, and how to read an amplitude off a map, then size the marks for a fan on a calculator.

Read the whole lesson as notes · every step, derivation and worked example, on one page

1Goal Where we are going

A spectrum says which frequencies are in a signal, not when. A wavelet map says both. This is the map of the fan from the video: a steady hum along the bottom, and a flash each time it knocks.

This lesson derives what that map shows and how to read it. The working builds up below the board; click any heading there to fold it away.

By the end of this lesson we will have derived what a wavelet map shows for the two things a machine signal is made of. A steady tone of amplitude AAA at f0f_0f0​ gives a band, the same at every time bbb:

← a tone

A sharp knock at t0t_0t0​ gives a flash whose length is set by the row:

← a knock

and every mark on the map has the same area, whatever its frequency:

Then we size the marks for the fan in the video, and read an amplitude off a map by hand.

3Derivation The wavelet and its copies

One wave inside a bell, slid to a time bbb and stretched by a scale aaa. The wavelet transform W(a,b)W(a,b)W(a,b) is how well the signal matches each copy.

4Derivation Each scale is a frequency

The arrow inside a copy turns at ω0/a\omega_0/aω0​/a radians per second, so the scale aaa and the frequency fff are the same thing in different units.

Every copy holds about one cycle per aaa: fast rows get a short burst, slow rows a long one.

Inside (4), the arrow’s angle is ω0(t−b)/a\omega_0(t-b)/aω0​(t−b)/a. It turns at ω0/a\omega_0/aω0​/a radians per second, so by (D)

For example, the 25 Hz row of a map uses

(6)

The bell in (4) is e−(t−b)2/(2a2)e^{-(t-b)^2/(2a^2)}e−(t−b)2/(2a2): its width is proportional to aaa. Cycles per second times seconds of bell:

So every copy holds the same number of wiggles. A fast row gets a short burst, a slow row a long one. Slide the frequency on the board.

6Derivation A sharp knock

A knock contains every frequency, so every row lights up, but each one for a time proportional to its scale. The map of one knock is a cone pointing at the moment it happened.

The board is (15), drawn for one knock at the centre.

Now the opposite kind of signal: one sharp knock at t0t_0t0​, idealised as an impulse of strength III:

x(t)=I δ(t−t0)x(t)=I\,\delta(t-t_0)x(t)=Iδ(t−t0​)
(13)

Put it into (4). By (F) the integral just evaluates the wavelet at t0t_0t0​:

Take the size:

∣W(a,b)∣=Ia e−(t0−b)2/(2a2)|W(a,b)|=\frac{I}{\sqrt a}\,e^{-(t_0-b)^2/(2a^2)}∣W(a,b)∣=a​I​e−(t0​−b)2/(2a2)

Every row lights up, because a knock contains every frequency. But each row lights only for a time proportional to its aaa: a short flash in the fast rows, a long smear in the slow ones. On the map a knock is a cone pointing at t0t_0t0​.

7What it means Every mark has the same area

Every mark on a wavelet map has the same area. What changes is its shape: tall and thin in the fast rows, short and wide in the slow ones. A short-time FFT uses one window for every row, so all its marks are the same shape. That is the whole difference.

8What it means Choosing ω₀

ω0\omega_0ω0​ sets how many wiggles sit under the bell. More wiggles: sharper in frequency, longer in time. The area of a mark does not change.

Below about 5 the wavelet no longer averages to zero. 6 is the usual choice.

Both widths contain ω0\omega_0ω0​. Put a=ω0/(2πf)a=\omega_0/(2\pi f)a=ω0​/(2πf) into (16), and look again at (18):

Δt=2.355 ω02πf=0.375 ω0f,Δff=2.355ω0\Delta t=\frac{2.355\,\omega_0}{2\pi f}=\frac{0.375\,\omega_0}{f},\qquad \frac{\Delta f}{f}=\frac{2.355}{\omega_0}Δt=2πf2.355ω0​​=f0.375ω0​​,fΔf​=ω0​2.355​
(20)

A bigger ω0\omega_0ω0​ puts more wiggles under the bell (7): sharper in frequency, blurrier in time. The product (19) does not move. It is the short-time FFT’s trade again, but set once as a fraction of fff, not as a fixed number of hertz.

Why not make ω0\omega_0ω0​ small? A wavelet must average to zero, or it answers a constant offset. Use (E) on (1) with κ=ω0\kappa=\omega_0κ=ω0​:

∫ψ(u) du=2π  e−ω02/2\int\psi(u)\,du=\sqrt{2\pi}\;e^{-\omega_0^2/2}∫ψ(u)du=2π​e−ω02​/2
(21)← 4×10⁻⁸ at ω₀ = 6, but 0.03 at ω₀ = 3

ω0=6\omega_0=6ω0​=6 is the usual choice (Torrence and Compo, 1998): as short as it can be while (21) stays negligible.
