Sines and cosines

A point going round a shaft, traced over time: amplitude, frequency, phase, and why a sine plus a cosine is one shifted sine.

1Goal Where we are going

Every vibration spectrum is built from sines and cosines. This lesson derives them from a point going round a shaft, the way you would in a maths class, then solves two examples on a calculator.

3Derivation A point going round

A shaft turns. Follow one point on it: its height, plotted against time, is a sine.

One full circle is one period TTT. The number of turns per second is the frequency fff, and ω=2πf\omega=2\pi fω=2πf is the same thing measured in radians per second.

A point on the shaft goes round a circle of radius AAA. At angle θ\thetaθ it is at

(x, y)=(Acos⁡θ, Asin⁡θ)(x,\,y)=(A\cos\theta,\ A\sin\theta)(x,y)=(Acosθ, Asinθ)
(1)← its height is the y part

The shaft turns steadily, so the angle grows at a constant rate ω\omegaω:

θ=ωt\theta=\omega tθ=ωt
(2)

Substituting (2) in the height from (1):

x(t)=Asin⁡(ωt)x(t)=A\sin(\omega t)x(t)=Asin(ωt)
(3)← height against time: a sine

One full turn is θ=2π\theta=2\piθ=2π, reached after one period TTT. From (2), ωT=2π\omega T=2\piωT=2π, so

ω=2πT=2πf\omega=\frac{2\pi}{T}=2\pi fω=T2π​=2πf
(4)← f = turns per second

4Derivation Amplitude and frequency

Amplitude AAA is how far the point swings. Frequency is how fast it goes round.

On a machine, frequencies are counted in multiples of running speed: 1X is once per revolution, 2X twice per revolution, and so on.

A feature that comes round nnn times per revolution has nnn times the angle rate. Putting ω=n ω0\omega=n\,\omega_0ω=nω0​ in (3):

x(t)=Asin⁡(n ω0t)x(t)=A\sin(n\,\omega_0 t)x(t)=Asin(nω0​t)
(5)← n = order
n=1: 1X,n=2: 2X,n=3: 3Xn=1:\ 1\text{X},\qquad n=2:\ 2\text{X},\qquad n=3:\ 3\text{X}n=1: 1X,n=2: 2X,n=3: 3X

5Derivation Phase: where it starts

Two points on the same shaft go round at the same speed but start at different angles. That head start is the phase φ\varphiφ. The dashed line is the same sine with zero phase.

A phase of 90° turns a sine into a cosine.

A point that starts at angle φ\varphiφ instead of 0 has θ=ωt+φ\theta=\omega t+\varphiθ=ωt+φ, so from (1)

x(t)=Asin⁡(ωt+φ)x(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\varphi)x(t)=Asin(ωt+φ)
(6)← φ is the phase

From (D), the angle-sum rule:

sin⁡(a+b)=sin⁡acos⁡b+cos⁡asin⁡b\sin(a+b)=\sin a\cos b+\cos a\sin bsin(a+b)=sinacosb+cosasinb
(7)

Put a=ωta=\omega ta=ωt, b=π2b=\tfrac{\pi}{2}b=2π​ in (7). We know that cos⁡π2=0\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}=0cos2π​=0 and sin⁡π2=1\sin\tfrac{\pi}{2}=1sin2π​=1:

sin⁡ ⁣(ωt+π2)=sin⁡ωt⋅0+cos⁡ωt⋅1=cos⁡(ωt)\sin\!\big(\omega t+\tfrac{\pi}{2}\big)=\sin\omega t\cdot 0+\cos\omega t\cdot 1=\cos(\omega t)sin(ωt+2π​)=sinωt⋅0+cosωt⋅1=cos(ωt)
(8)← a cosine is a sine, 90° ahead

6Derivation A sine plus a cosine

Add a cosine and a sine at the same frequency and you get one sine, shifted. Its size is c=a2+b2c=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}c=a2+b2​, like the long side of a right triangle.

So any amount of shift can be written as a mix of a sine and a cosine. That's why a spectrum lane holds both, and why the peak height is ccc.

Expand csin⁡(ωt+φ)c\sin(\omega t+\varphi)csin(ωt+φ) using (7):

csin⁡(ωt+φ)=ccos⁡φ sin⁡ωt+csin⁡φ cos⁡ωtc\sin(\omega t+\varphi)=c\cos\varphi\,\sin\omega t+c\sin\varphi\,\cos\omega tcsin(ωt+φ)=ccosφsinωt+csinφcosωt
(9)

For (9) to equal acos⁡ωt+bsin⁡ωta\cos\omega t+b\sin\omega tacosωt+bsinωt at every ttt, the parts must match:

b=ccos⁡φ,a=csin⁡φb=c\cos\varphi,\qquad a=c\sin\varphib=ccosφ,a=csinφ
(10)

Square both parts of (10) and add. From (B), sin⁡2φ+cos⁡2φ=1\sin^2\varphi+\cos^2\varphi=1sin2φ+cos2φ=1:

a2+b2=c2(sin⁡2φ+cos⁡2φ)=c2a^2+b^2=c^2\big(\sin^2\varphi+\cos^2\varphi\big)=c^2a2+b2=c2(sin2φ+cos2φ)=c2
(11)

Taking the square root of (11), and dividing the two parts of (10):

c=a2+b2,tan⁡φ=abc=\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\qquad \tan\varphi=\frac{a}{b}c=a2+b2​,tanφ=ba​
(12)← size and phase

7Derivation Orders: from rpm to hertz

Vibration analysts name frequencies by order: multiples of running speed. A pump at 1490 rpm turns 1490/60≈24.81490/60\approx 24.81490/60≈24.8 times a second, so 1X is 24.8 Hz, 2X is 49.7 Hz, 3X is 74.5 Hz.

Next lesson: why a repeating signal can only contain these whole orders.

Running speed in rpm is revolutions per minute. Dividing by 60 seconds per minute gives revolutions per second, which is 1X in hertz:

f1=rpm60f_1=\frac{\text{rpm}}{60}f1​=60rpm​
(13)

By (5), the nnnth order turns nnn times faster:

fn=n f1f_n=n\,f_1fn​=nf1​
(14)

For example, at 1490 rpm, (13) and (14) give

f1=149060≈24.8 Hz,f2≈49.7 Hz,f3≈74.5 Hzf_1=\frac{1490}{60}\approx 24.8\ \text{Hz},\qquad f_2\approx 49.7\ \text{Hz},\qquad f_3\approx 74.5\ \text{Hz}f1​=601490​≈24.8 Hz,f2​≈49.7 Hz,f3​≈74.5 Hz
