1Goal Where we are going

An analyser turns 8, 1024 or 65 536 samples into a spectrum. This lesson derives the formula it uses, from scratch, the way you would in a maths class.

Then we solve two examples on a calculator, every operation written out, and you try one yourself.

2What we already know The tools

Four facts carry the whole derivation: the Fourier series (1), three product-to-sum identities (2)–(4), zero net area over whole cycles (5)–(6), and Euler’s formula (7).

On the board: any whole number of cycles has as much area above the line as below. Change the number of cycles.

As we know, any signal that repeats every TTT seconds can be written as a Fourier series: a constant plus cosines and sines at whole multiples of the running speed:

x(t)=a0+∑m=1∞[amcos⁡(mω0t)+bmsin⁡(mω0t)],ω0=2πTx(t)=a_0+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\big[a_m\cos(m\omega_0 t)+b_m\sin(m\omega_0 t)\big],\qquad \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T}x(t)=a0​+m=1∑∞​[am​cos(mω0​t)+bm​sin(mω0​t)],ω0​=T2π​
(1)← the harmonics

From trigonometry, a product of two cosines or sines turns into a sum:

(2)
sin⁡Acos⁡B=12[sin⁡(A+B)+sin⁡(A−B)]\sin A\cos B=\tfrac12\big[\sin(A+B)+\sin(A-B)\big]sinAcosB=21​[sin(A+B)+sin(A−B)]

3Derivation Orthogonality

The key fact: multiply two different harmonics and the result has zero net area over a period. Multiply a harmonic by itself and you get T/2T/2T/2.

Set nnn and mmm on the board. The orange curve is their product; the green and red areas cancel unless n=mn=mn=m.

Take two harmonics, nnn and mmm (whole numbers, both at least 1), and integrate their product over one period. Using (2) with A=nω0tA=n\omega_0tA=nω0​t, B=mω0tB=m\omega_0tB=mω0​t:

∫0Tcos⁡(nω0t)cos⁡(mω0t) dt=12∫0Tcos⁡((n−m)ω0t)dt+12∫0Tcos⁡((n+m)ω0t)dt\int_0^T\cos(n\omega_0t)\cos(m\omega_0t)\,dt=\tfrac12\int_0^T\cos\big((n-m)\omega_0t\big)dt+\tfrac12\int_0^T\cos\big((n+m)\omega_0t\big)dt∫0T​cos(nω0​t)cos(mω0​t)dt=21​∫0T​cos((n−m)ω0​t)dt+21​∫0T​cos((n+m)ω0​t)dt
(8)

n+m≥2n+m\geq2n+m≥2, so the second integral is 0 by (5). The first is also 0 by (5), unless n=mn=mn=m: then it is cos⁡0\cos 0cos0, which gives TTT by (6). So

∫0Tcos⁡(nω0t)cos⁡(mω0t) dt={T/2,n=m0,n≠m\int_0^T\cos(n\omega_0t)\cos(m\omega_0t)\,dt=\begin{cases}T/2, & n=m\\ 0, & n\neq m\end{cases}∫0T​cos(nω0​t)cos(mω0​t)dt={T/2,0,​n=mn=m​
(9)

Exactly the same steps with (4) and (3):

∫0Tsin⁡(nω0t)sin⁡(mω0t) dt={T/2,n=m0,n≠m\int_0^T\sin(n\omega_0t)\sin(m\omega_0t)\,dt=\begin{cases}T/2, & n=m\\ 0, & n\neq m\end{cases}∫0T​sin(nω0​t)sin(mω0​t)dt={T/2,0,​n=mn=m​
(10)
∫0Tsin⁡(nω0t)cos⁡(mω0t) dt=0for all n,m\int_0^T\sin(n\omega_0t)\cos(m\omega_0t)\,dt=0\quad\text{for all }n,m∫0T​sin(nω0​t)cos(mω0​t)dt=0for all n,m
(11)← by (3): both terms are sines, 0 by (5)

This is called orthogonality: two different harmonics, or a sine and a cosine, multiply to zero net area. Only a harmonic times itself survives.

5Derivation One complex number

6Derivation From the integral to a sum

An analyser has samples, not a curve. Replace the integral by NNN thin rectangles, one per sample, and it becomes the DFT, the formula every FFT computes.

On the board, the area under x(t)cos⁡(ω0t)x(t)\cos(\omega_0t)x(t)cos(ω0​t) and the 8 rectangles give the same answer.

An analyser never has the curve, only samples. Take NNN equally spaced samples in one period:

tk=kTN,xk=x(tk),dt→TN,k=0,1,…,N−1t_k=\frac{kT}{N},\qquad x_k=x(t_k),\qquad dt\to\frac{T}{N},\qquad k=0,1,\dots,N-1tk​=NkT​,xk​=x(tk​),dt→NT​,k=0,1,…,N−1

In (17), the integral becomes a sum of NNN thin rectangles, each T/NT/NT/N wide:

7Derivation Why half is a mirror

Bin N−nN-nN−n is bin nnn wound the other way: the complex conjugate. It carries no new information, so analysers drop the top half and double the rest.

Slide through the bins: 7 mirrors 1, 6 mirrors 2.
