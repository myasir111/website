Build a signal from harmonics

Why only whole multiples of running speed fit a repeating signal, and how sines and cosines add up to any shape.

Read the whole lesson as notes · every step, derivation and worked example, on one page

1Goal Where we are going

Any repeating vibration can be built from sines and cosines at whole multiples of running speed. This lesson proves it, derives the formulas that measure each harmonic, and works two shapes out in full.

By the end of this lesson we will have shown why a repeating signal contains only whole orders, and derived the formulas that measure each one:

3Derivation A signal that repeats

This is a vibration signal from a rotating machine: a bit of imbalance, a bit of misalignment, and an impact once every revolution that rings and dies away.

It looks messy, but it does one important thing: it repeats every turn. If one revolution takes TTT seconds, the signal at t+Tt+Tt+T is the same as at ttt.

A machine turns once every TTT seconds, so its vibration repeats every turn:

f(t+T)=f(t)f(t+T)=f(t)f(t+T)=f(t)
(1)← repeats every turn

One revolution takes

T=60rpm s,ω0=2πT⇒ω0T=2πT=\frac{60}{\text{rpm}}\ \text{s},\qquad \omega_0=\frac{2\pi}{T}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \omega_0 T=2\piT=rpm60​ s,ω0​=T2π​⇒ω0​T=2π
(2)← ω₀ is running speed in rad/s

4Derivation Which sines fit one turn?

Any sine that is part of this signal must repeat every turn too. Try a sine at mmm times running speed. The dashed copy is the same sine one turn later; the shading shows where they disagree.

Only whole numbers line up. That's why spectrum peaks sit at exactly 1X, 2X, 3X… and never at 2.3X.

Take a sine at mmm times running speed and shift it by one turn:

sin⁡(mω0(t+T))=sin⁡(mω0t+m ω0T)\sin\big(m\omega_0(t+T)\big)=\sin(m\omega_0 t+m\,\omega_0 T)sin(mω0​(t+T))=sin(mω0​t+mω0​T)
← multiply out

Substituting ω0T=2π\omega_0 T=2\piω0​T=2π from (2):

sin⁡(mω0(t+T))=sin⁡(mω0t+2πm)\sin\big(m\omega_0(t+T)\big)=\sin(m\omega_0 t+2\pi m)sin(mω0​(t+T))=sin(mω0​t+2πm)
(3)

By (A), a sine repeats every 2π2\pi2π, and only then. So for (3) to equal sin⁡(mω0t)\sin(m\omega_0 t)sin(mω0​t) at every ttt, 2πm2\pi m2πm must be a whole number of turns:

m=1,2,3,…m=1,2,3,\dotsm=1,2,3,…
(4)← only whole multiples fit

6Derivation Sines and cosines in each lane

Real signals don't all start at zero, so each lane holds a cosine as well as a sine. Together they make one sine that is shifted in time.

The spectrum shows only the combined height cnc_ncn​; the shift φn\varphi_nφn​ is the phase. That's why we say a signal is a sum of sines and cosines.

A lane can hold a cosine as well. By (B):

ancos⁡(nω0t)+bnsin⁡(nω0t)=cnsin⁡(nω0t+φn)a_n\cos(n\omega_0 t)+b_n\sin(n\omega_0 t)=c_n\sin(n\omega_0 t+\varphi_n)an​cos(nω0​t)+bn​sin(nω0​t)=cn​sin(nω0​t+φn​)
(6)
cn=an2+bn2,tan⁡φn=anbnc_n=\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2},\qquad \tan\varphi_n=\frac{a_n}{b_n}cn​=an2​+bn2​​,tanφn​=bn​an​​
(7)← cₙ is the peak you see

7Derivation Build a shape, harmonic by harmonic

Pick a shape and add harmonics one at a time. Sharp jumps (square, sawtooth) need many harmonics and their peaks fall slowly, like 1/n1/n1/n. Corners only (triangle) fall like 1/n21/n^21/n2, so a few terms already look right.

Near a jump the sum always overshoots by about 9% of the jump, however many terms you add: the Gibbs phenomenon.

How much nnnX is inside fff? Multiply by that sine and average over one turn (why this works: step 8):

8Derivation Back to the machine

The same works for the messy machine signal. Add its harmonics one by one: 1X gives the imbalance, 2X the misalignment, and the sharp impact needs the higher harmonics around 4X–7X.

Every peak in a spectrum is one of these lanes. The harmonics aren't extra signals: they're the shape, written as sines and cosines.

Putting sines and cosines of every lane together, any repeating signal can be written as

f(t)=a0+∑n=1∞[ancos⁡(nω0t)+bnsin⁡(nω0t)]f(t)=a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big[a_n\cos(n\omega_0 t)+b_n\sin(n\omega_0 t)\big]f(t)=a0​+n=1∑∞​[an​cos(nω0​t)+bn​sin(nω0​t)]
(15)← the Fourier series

Each coefficient is found as in (8). Multiplying by a sine or cosine and averaging over a turn keeps only the matching lane, because by (E) all the others average to zero:

a0=1T ⁣∫0T ⁣f(t) dta_0=\frac{1}{T}\!\int_0^T\! f(t)\,dta0​=T1​∫0T​f(t)dt
(16)← the DC level
an=2T ⁣∫0T ⁣f(t)cos⁡(nω0t) dt,bn=2T ⁣∫0T ⁣f(t)sin⁡(nω0t) dta_n=\frac{2}{T}\!\int_0^T\! f(t)\cos(n\omega_0 t)\,dt,\qquad b_n=\frac{2}{T}\!\int_0^T\! f(t)\sin(n\omega_0 t)\,dtan​=T2​∫0T​f(t)cos(nω0​t)dt,bn​=T2​∫0T​f(t)sin(nω0​t)dt
(17)

An FFT computes these integrals from sampled data: that is what the analyser does.
