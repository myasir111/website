A mass on a spring

Pull it down, let go, and it keeps its own time. Why that rhythm, why the swing size doesn’t change it, and how the sag of a machine on its mounts gives its natural frequency.

Read the whole lesson as notes · every step, derivation and worked example, on one page

1The puzzle Why does it keep its own time?

Hang a mass on a spring, pull it down and let go. It bounces at a rhythm of its own. Pull it twice as far and it swings twice as wide, yet the rhythm doesn't change.

Why not? And why does a heavier mass bounce slower, but not twice as slow for twice the mass?

By the end of this lesson we will have answered:

Why does a mass on a spring bounce at a rhythm of its own?

Why doesn’t pulling it further change that rhythm?

How does the rhythm depend on the mass and the stiffness, and why a square root?

Then we solve two examples with every operation shown, and you try one yourself.

3Derivation Newton meets Hooke

Put the spring's force into F=maF=maF=ma and you get one equation: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement, and opposite to it.

Watch the arrow: biggest at the ends of the swing, zero as the mass passes rest.

Measure xxx from where the mass hangs at rest, positive upward. Gravity is already balanced there by the stretch at rest, so the only extra force is the spring’s pull back, from (A). Put it into (B):

m a=−k xm\,a=-k\,xma=−kx
(1)

Write the acceleration with (C):

m d2xdt2=−k x⇒d2xdt2=−km xm\,\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-k\,x\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-\frac{k}{m}\,xmdt2d2x​=−kx⇒dt2d2x​=−mk​x
(2)← the equation of motion

In words: the acceleration is always proportional to the displacement, and always points back towards rest.

4Derivation Guess a cosine

Which shape is its own second derivative, flipped? A cosine. The dashed orange line is the acceleration: the same curve, upside down.

That only fits the equation for one value of ω\omegaω, and it's set by kkk and mmm.

5Derivation Why the swing size doesn’t matter

Drag the amplitude. The swing gets bigger or smaller, and the rhythm stays exactly the same.

The amplitude cancels out of the equation, so it can't set the frequency.

The amplitude AAA appears on both sides of (4), so it cancels. Nothing in (5) depends on AAA:

ω depends on k and m only, not on A\omega\ \text{depends on}\ k\ \text{and}\ m\ \text{only, not on}\ Aω depends on k and m only, not on A
← pull it twice as far: same rhythm

That only works because the force in (A) grows in step with the stretch. A spring that stiffens as it stretches would change its rhythm with amplitude.

6Derivation Heavier is slower, by a square root

7Derivation How fast, how hard

Differentiate the cosine and you get the velocity (green), then the acceleration (orange, dashed). The mass moves fastest as it passes rest and pulls hardest at the ends.

This is why displacement, velocity and acceleration readings convert into each other at one frequency. The unit converter does exactly this.

Differentiate (3) once with (D) for the velocity, and once more for the acceleration:

v=dxdt=−ωAsin⁡(ωt),a=d2xdt2=−ω2Acos⁡(ωt)v=\frac{dx}{dt}=-\omega A\sin(\omega t),\qquad a=\frac{d^2x}{dt^2}=-\omega^2A\cos(\omega t)v=dtdx​=−ωAsin(ωt),a=dt2d2x​=−ω2Acos(ωt)
(8)

The biggest values come when the sine or cosine is ±1:

(9)← the A-level pair

Velocity peaks as the mass passes rest; acceleration peaks at the ends of the swing, pointing back. At one frequency the three readings an analyser can give are tied together:

8Derivation The energy only swaps

The orange bar is the energy stored in the spring; the green bar is the energy of the moving mass. One fills as the other empties, and the total never changes.

Setting the two maxima equal gives the natural frequency a second way, with no differential equation. Engineers call it Rayleigh's method; it is how beams and plates get their frequencies later in this track.

The spring stores 12kx2\tfrac12kx^221​kx2 and the moving mass carries 12mv2\tfrac12mv^221​mv2. Add them, using (3) and (8):

12kx2+12mv2=12kA2cos⁡2ωt+12mω2A2sin⁡2ωt\tfrac12kx^2+\tfrac12mv^2=\tfrac12kA^2\cos^2\omega t+\tfrac12m\omega^2A^2\sin^2\omega t21​kx2+21​mv2=21​kA2cos2ωt+21​mω2A2sin2ωt
(11)

From (5), mω2=km\omega^2=kmω2=k, and sin⁡2+cos⁡2=1\sin^2+\cos^2=1sin2+cos2=1:

12kx2+12mv2=12kA2(cos⁡2ωt+sin⁡2ωt)=12kA2\tfrac12kx^2+\tfrac12mv^2=\tfrac12kA^2\left(\cos^2\omega t+\sin^2\omega t\right)=\tfrac12kA^221​kx2+21​mv2=21​kA2(cos2ωt+sin2ωt)=21​kA2
(12)← the same at every instant

So the energy only swaps between spring and mass. Turned round, this is Rayleigh’s method: set the largest motion energy equal to the largest spring energy, and the frequency comes out without solving any equation:

12m (ωA)2=12kA2⇒ω2=km\tfrac12m\,(\omega A)^2=\tfrac12kA^2\quad\Rightarrow\quad\omega^2=\frac km21​m(ωA)2=21​kA2⇒ω2=mk​
(13)← (5) again, a second way

Pull it to x0x_0x0​ and also give it a push v0v_0v0​. A cosine plus a sine fits both starting values, and from Lesson S1 it is one sine of size

9Derivation The sag shortcut

On site you rarely know kkk and mmm, but you can measure how far a machine sinks into its mounts. That sag alone gives the natural frequency.

1 mm of sag means about 15.8 Hz. Four times the sag halves the frequency.

At rest, the spring holds the weight. If that weight stretches it by δ\deltaδ, from (A) the stretch force equals the weight:

k δ=m g⇒km=gδk\,\delta=m\,g\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{k}{m}=\frac{g}{\delta}kδ=mg⇒mk​=δg​
(15)

Put (15) in (6). The mass and the stiffness drop out; only the sag is left:

vmax⁡=ωnA,amax⁡=ωn2Av_{\max}=\omega_nA,\qquad a_{\max}=\omega_n^2Avmax​=ωn​A,amax​=ωn2​A
← from (9)
fn=12πgδf_n=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{g}{\delta}}fn​=2π1​δg​​
(16)

With g=9.81g=9.81g=9.81 m/s² and δ\deltaδ in millimetres, 9810/2π=15.76\sqrt{9810}/2\pi=15.769810​/2π=15.76:

fn≈15.76δmm Hzf_n\approx\frac{15.76}{\sqrt{\delta_{\text{mm}}}}\ \text{Hz}fn​≈δmm​​15.76​ Hz
(17)← sag 1 mm: 15.8 Hz

Practice

Three problems: a warm-up, a stretch and one from site. Try each before you open the answer.

  1. Warm-up

    A 2 kg mass hangs on a spring of 800 N/m. What is its natural frequency?

    Show the answer

  2. Stretch

    You double the mass on the same spring. By what factor does fnf_nfn​ change? What mass would halve it?

    Show the answer
    From fn∝1/mf_n\propto1/\sqrt mfn​∝1/m​: doubling the mass multiplies fnf_nfn​ by 1/2≈0.711/\sqrt2\approx0.711/2​≈0.71. To halve it you need m′/m=2\sqrt{m\prime/m}=2m′/m​=2, so four times the mass.

  3. On site

    A pump skid sinks 5 mm into its mounts. The motor runs at 1,480 rpm. How close is running speed to the natural frequency on the mounts?
