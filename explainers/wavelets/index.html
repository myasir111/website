Wavelets: which frequency, and when

November 2026 · 1 min 41 s film, with music · interactive

The film from the LinkedIn post. No voice: captions are on screen.

What the film shows. A fan knocks once every turn. Two different signals, one playing 10 Hz then 25 Hz and the other playing the same thing backwards, have exactly the same FFT spectrum: a spectrum says which frequencies, not when. Short FFTs slid along the signal bring time back, but one window size can't be sharp in time and in frequency at once. A wavelet is a short burst that is slid along the signal and stretched: short for fast things, long for slow ones. On the fan, the hum is a steady band and each knock is a flash, 40 ms apart.

WAVELETS

Which, and when

A map of frequency against time. Short bursts for fast things, long ones for slow things.

Two signals, one spectrum

The FFT matches a signal against an arrow turning at each frequency, over the whole record:

$$X(f)=\int x(t)\,e^{-i2\pi ft}\,dt.$$

Play a record backwards and the spectrum's size doesn't change. For a real signal on \(0\le t\le T\), the reversed record \(x(T-t)\) has the transform \(e^{-i2\pi fT}\,\overline{X(f)}\): only the phase moves, so \(|X(f)|\) is identical. The order of events lives only in the phase, and a magnitude spectrum, which is what an analyser shows, throws it away.

That is fine for a steady machine. It isn't fine for something that comes and goes: an impact once per turn, a run-up, a valve that chatters for a second.

First try: short FFTs

Slide a short window \(w\) along the signal and take an FFT of each slice:

$$X(\tau,f)=\int x(t)\,w(t-\tau)\,e^{-i2\pi ft}\,dt.$$

That is the spectrogram, and it does bring time back. But the window has one length for every frequency. A short window locates events sharply and blurs frequency; a long one does the opposite. The trade can't be beaten: for any window, the spreads in time and frequency (as standard deviations of the window's energy) obey

$$\sigma_t\,\sigma_f\ \ge\ \frac{1}{4\pi},$$

with equality only for a Gaussian window (Gabor, 1946).

The idea: a wavelet

A wavelet is a short wave inside a bell. The one used in the film is the Morlet wavelet, a cosine-and-sine arrow under a Gaussian:

$$\psi(t)=e^{-t^2/2}\,e^{i\omega_0t},\qquad \omega_0=6.$$

Slide it to a time \(b\), stretch it by a scale \(a\), and match it against the signal:

$$W(a,b)=\frac{1}{\sqrt a}\int x(t)\,\psi^{*}\!\Big(\frac{t-b}{a}\Big)\,dt.$$

Each scale is a frequency, \(f=\omega_0/(2\pi a)\), and every stretched copy holds the same number of wiggles. So a fast row of the map uses a short burst and a slow row a long one. Every mark on the map has the same area in time and frequency; what changes is its shape.

On a machine: one knock per turn

The film ends on a fan at 1,500 rpm: a 25 Hz hum, and a small knock once per turn that rings a resonance near 400 Hz. One turn takes

$$\frac{60\ \text{s}}{1500\ \text{turns}}=0.040\ \text{s}.$$

On the wavelet map the hum is a steady band and each knock is a flash. At 400 Hz the wavelet is short, so each flash lasts about 5.6 ms (at half height) and the knocks, 40 ms apart, stand out one by one. At 25 Hz the wavelet is long, so the same knock would smear over about 90 ms, but that row pins the hum to 25 ± 5 Hz. A single short-time FFT has to pick one of those two compromises for the whole map.

Reading a wavelet map in practice

Brightness is not amplitude. With the \(1/\sqrt a\) used here, a steady tone of amplitude \(A\) reads \(\tfrac{A}{2}\sqrt{2\pi a}\), so slow rows look brighter than they are. Some tools scale by \(1/a\) instead, which makes equal amplitudes equally bright. Check which one your software uses before comparing rows.

Watch the edges. Near the start and end of a record the long, slow wavelets hang off the end of the data, so the slow rows are unreliable there. Torrence and Compo call this region the cone of influence; good tools shade it.

Where it helps. Anything that changes during the record: impacts and rubs that come once per turn, run-ups and coast-downs, intermittent faults. For a steady machine an ordinary averaged spectrum is still the sharper tool.
