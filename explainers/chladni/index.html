Chladni plates: every peak has a shape

November 2026 · 2 min film, with music · four boards to play with

The same film as the LinkedIn post. No voice: captions are on screen. Music: “Interstellar Piano” by Ivan_Luzan (Pixabay).

What the film shows. A square aluminium plate, 240 mm across and 1 mm thick, bolted at its centre to a shaker and covered in sand. At 967 Hz the sand runs off almost everywhere and gathers into a ring and four curved lines. Then the film builds the reason, one piece at a time: why a grain leaves the plate, why only some frequencies make a pattern, why every peak has a shape, and why a sensor on a still line misses it. This page does the same, with a board for each step.

ON A SAND LINE

Sees nothing

The plate does not move there in that mode. A sensor there misses the mode’s peak, and a brace there leaves the mode exactly where it was. Easy to miss, easy to waste a brace on.

OFF THE LINES

Sees it, moves it

Where the mode moves, a sensor reads its peak and a brace changes it: the mode no longer fits and its frequency moves. Measure at more than one point; brace where it moves most.

1. Why the sand moves

Put one grain on the plate and shake it gently. The grain rides along: going up, the plate pushes it; going down, gravity keeps it pressed on. Now shake harder. On the way down the plate can start falling faster than gravity can pull the grain after it, and the grain is left in the air. Drag the board past 1 g and watch the moment it happens, where the plate’s acceleration (right) dips below −g.

A grain on a shaking plate · slowed right down

Grain: …

Now the formula, which only says what the board shows. A point of the plate moves as \(w=W\cos\omega t\); differentiate twice and its acceleration peaks at \(\omega^2|W|\). The grain leaves when that beats gravity:

$$\omega^2|W|>g\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad|W|>\frac{g}{\omega^2}.$$

At 967 Hz that is \(9.81/(2\pi\times967)^2=0.27\ \mu\text{m}\), a few hundredths of a hair’s width; one micrometre is already \(3.8\,g\). So almost the whole plate throws its sand. Where the plate moves a lot the grains hop far, where it barely moves they hardly hop, so they wander until they land somewhere still and stay there. The stillest places are the lines where \(W=0\), the nodal lines. The sand draws them.

2. Two waves, one fixed shape

The shaker sends bending waves out to the edges, and they bounce back. A wave and its echo travelling the other way add up, with \(\sin(a-b)+\sin(a+b)=2\sin a\cos b\), to

$$\begin{aligned}&\sin(kx-\omega t)+\sin(kx+\omega t)\\&\qquad=2\sin kx\,\cos\omega t:\end{aligned}$$

a shape that stays put, times a clock. Nothing travels any more, and wherever \(\sin kx=0\) the plate never moves at all. That is the same “shape times clock” split as the harmonics in What the FFT actually shows, spread over a plate instead of over time.

3. Why only some frequencies

A wave doesn’t bounce once: it goes back and forth, and every round trip leaves another copy on top of the last. If each copy comes back in step with the one before, they pile up into a big standing wave. If not, they land out of step and cancel. Slide the wavelength and watch the white total: it only grows big when a whole number of half-waves fits between the edges.

Echoes on a strip held at both ends · orange: each round trip · white: their total

The echoes: …

In formulas: a round trip is a distance \(2L\), which shifts the wave’s phase by \(2kL\). Back in step means a whole number of turns:

$$\begin{gathered}2kL=2\pi m\\\Downarrow\\k_m=\frac{m\pi}{L},\qquad m=1,2,3,\dots\end{gathered}$$

The plate decides how fast each of those waves rings. Newton’s law for a small patch of plate, with the bending stiffness \(D=Eh^3/12(1-\nu^2)\) and the mass per square metre \(\rho h\), gives the plate equation; put in a wave \(W=\sin kx\) and its four derivatives bring back \(k^4\):

$$\begin{gathered}D\nabla^4W=\rho h\,\omega^2W\\\Downarrow\\Dk^4=\rho h\,\omega^2\\\Downarrow\\\omega_m=\Big(\frac{m\pi}{L}\Big)^2\sqrt{\frac{D}{\rho h}}.\end{gathered}$$

So the fits ring at a ladder of frequencies, \(1, 4, 9, 16\dots\) times the first, with the gaps growing. It also explains why higher patterns are busier: the wavelength only shrinks as \(1/\sqrt f\), from 100 mm at 967 Hz to 53 mm at 3,447 Hz on the film’s plate (\(D=6.45\) N m, \(\rho h=2.7\) kg/m²). A real plate with free edges and a bolted centre has no neat formula, but the idea is the same, and its fits are 2D shapes. Walther Ritz first computed them accurately in 1909, with the method this film’s solver still uses.

4. Why a peak

Each of those fits, a mode, behaves like a mass on a spring with a damper. Push it slowly and it just follows the push. Push it too fast and it can’t keep up. Push it in time with its own swing and every push adds a little energy, until the damper takes out as much as each push puts in. Drag the push rate and watch the dot draw the peak.

One mode, pushed · damping 0.08 here so the peak fits on screen

Swing: …

The same in one line of maths: the mode’s motion \(q\) obeys \(\ddot q+2\zeta\omega_n\dot q+\omega_n^2q=f\cos\omega t\). Try \(q\) swinging at the push rate and the size of the swing comes out as

$$|Q|=\frac{f}{\sqrt{(\omega_n^2-\omega^2)^2+(2\zeta\omega_n\omega)^2}},$$

and right at the mode’s own rate the first bracket vanishes:

$$\omega=\omega_n:\quad|Q|=\frac{1}{2\zeta}\,\frac{f}{\omega_n^2}.$$

In time, only the damping holds it back. The film’s plate has \(\zeta\approx0.005\), so its peaks stand \(1/2\zeta=100\) times above the push. A whole plate is many modes at once, each weighted by its own shape \(W_n\) at the point you look at:

$$a(x,\omega)\propto\sum_n\frac{\Gamma_n\,W_n(x)\,\omega^2}{\omega_n^2-\omega^2+2i\zeta\omega_n\omega}.$$

Near each \(\omega_n\) one term is huge: a peak in the spectrum, and that mode’s shape on the plate. Between peaks every term is small and the sand sits still. That is the whole film in one line: every peak in a spectrum is a shape somewhere.

At 967 Hz, with the sensor 63 mm out, it sits on the ring: its spectrum has no peak at 967 Hz at all, while 30 mm further in it reads a tall one. 6. Where you measure, where you brace

The sensor. The plate’s motion is a sum of modes, \(w(x,t)=\sum_n W_n(x)\,q_n(t)\), so a sensor at \(x\) reads every mode multiplied by that mode’s shape where it sits. On a nodal line of the 967 Hz mode, \(W_{967}(x)=0\): that term is zero at every frequency, and the 967 Hz peak is simply not there. On the film’s plate, two points 30 mm apart read 24 dB apart at 967 Hz.

The brace. Take a strip held at both ends and pin its middle. Its second mode already has a still point there, so the pin changes nothing: same shape, same frequency. Its first mode moved most right there, so it can’t keep its shape; it has to become two half-length humps, and its frequency jumps to four times what it was. A brace on a nodal line does nothing for that mode; a brace where the mode moves most moves it.

Duct walls, enclosure panels, skid decks and machine covers are plates. The same sums apply, with different edges.
