Sidebands: why every peak has twins

November 2026 · 2½ min film, with music · interactive

The film from the LinkedIn post. No voice: captions are on screen. Music: “Fjeld” by Alexander Nakarada, filmmusic.io Standard License.

What the film shows. A gearbox in slow motion: a 16-tooth pinion at 1,500 rpm whose shaft sits a hair off-centre, so the mesh bites harder, then softer, once per turn. A sensor hears 400 Hz swelling once per turn, and the spectrum shows a twin 25 Hz either side. Pulled apart in 3D, the swelling tone is three steady tones; seen end-on, each is one line of the spectrum, as in What the FFT actually shows. The wobble is two arrows spinning opposite ways, so the twins are mirror images, and it rides on every harmonic. Small, steady twins are normal. A waterfall of monthly spectra shows them growing with wear, then multiplying when a tooth cracks; its knock is a flash every 40 ms on a wavelet map and a crowd of sidebands 25 Hz apart in the spectrum. The gap names the part that wobbles.

NORMAL

Small and steady

Nothing is made perfect. Every gear is a little off-centre and every load wobbles, so every gearbox shows some sidebands. Trend them; don’t panic at them.

WORTH A LOOK

Growing or multiplying

Taller twins: the wobble got stronger. More of them, spread wide: something has started to knock. Their gap says which part.

Why they are there

Take a fast tone at \(\omega=2\pi f_c\), the gear mesh, and let its size rise and fall by a fraction \(m\) once per turn of \(\Omega=2\pi f_m\). That is a product, and a product of cosines is a sum of cosines:

$$(1+m\cos\Omega t)\cos\omega t=\cos\omega t+\tfrac m2\cos(\omega+\Omega)t+\tfrac m2\cos(\omega-\Omega)t.$$

Three steady tones: the carrier, and two twins at \(f_c\pm f_m\), each \(m/2\) as tall. Nothing extra is vibrating; the twins are the wobble, written as sines and cosines. Radio engineers call this amplitude modulation. In decibels each twin sits \(20\log_{10}(m/2)\) below the carrier: a 10% wobble puts them 26 dB down.

Why so even

The gap is \(f_m\), the frequency of whatever does the wobbling, and nothing else: move the carrier and the twins ride along at the same distance. They are equally tall because a cosine wobble is two equal arrows turning opposite ways,

$$1+m\cos\Omega t=1+\tfrac m2e^{i\Omega t}+\tfrac m2e^{-i\Omega t},$$

and multiplying by the carrier moves one arrow up to \(\omega+\Omega\) and the other down to \(\omega-\Omega\). This is the mirror half of the spectrum from Part 1, carried up to the mesh frequency.

When the timing wobbles

An off-centre pinion also makes its teeth arrive a little early, then a little late. The tone’s angle swings by up to \(\beta\) radians, so its frequency swings by \(\pm\beta f_m\). That is frequency modulation, and its spectrum is a ladder:

$$\cos(\omega t+\beta\sin\Omega t)=\sum_{n}J_n(\beta)\cos(\omega+n\Omega)t,$$

where \(J_n\) are Bessel functions. The rungs are still \(f_m\) apart. A small wobble gives one pair, \(\beta/2\) tall, with one of them flipped; a large one gives many. A real off-centre gear does both at once, and for small wobbles the twins become \((m+\beta)/2\) and \((m-\beta)/2\): one side grows, the other shrinks. Uneven twins mean both a size and a timing wobble.

Every harmonic gets them

A gear mesh is no pure sine: it repeats, so it has harmonics (Harmonics, Part 2). A size wobble multiplies all of it, so every harmonic gets twins \(f_m\) away in the same proportion. A timing wobble shifts time itself, so harmonic \(k\) swings \(k\) times the angle, \(\beta_k=k\beta\): higher harmonics grow wider ladders.

Start with the size wobble alone: one even pair. Add a timing wobble and the pair goes uneven; push it up and the ladder grows. Turn on the knock, a 1 ms pulse once per turn like a cracked tooth: a short event in time spreads into a wide family of lines, all the wobble’s frequency apart. That is the wavelets trade-off from Part 3, short in time and wide in frequency.

Reading sidebands on a machine

Are they normal? Yes, when they are small and steady. Nothing is made perfectly round or perfectly loaded, so a healthy gearbox shows a little modulation. A single spectrum says little on its own.

When to worry. Trend the sideband levels, not just the mesh line. Twins that grow month by month mean the wobble is getting stronger (wear, eccentricity, looseness). A family that suddenly spreads wide, many lines at the same gap, means something has started to knock once per turn, such as a cracked or chipped tooth. Uneven twins mean the timing wobbles as well as the size.

Where to look. The gap is the frequency of whatever wobbles:

AroundGapPoints to
Gear mesh frequency (teeth × shaft speed)Shaft speed of one gearThat gear: eccentricity, wear, a damaged tooth
Bearing inner-race frequency (BPFI)Shaft speedAn inner-race defect turning in and out of the load zone
Supply frequency, in the motor current\(2sf\) (twice the slip frequency)Broken rotor bars: lines at \(f(1\pm2s)\)

Seeing them at all. Twins \(f_m\) apart only separate when the record holds more than two turns of the wobble with a Hann window, \(T>2/f_m\); shorter, and leakage (Part 2) blurs them into one lump. Gear-shaft twins at 12.5 Hz need at least 0.16 s; broken-bar twins 2 Hz from a large 50 Hz line need far more than 1 s.
