The puzzle

A square wave, a sharp spike and something that looks like noise all give the same amplitude spectrum. The shape went somewhere: into the half of the FFT that the spectrum doesn't show.

Every frequency is an arrow

For each frequency, the FFT returns a complex number: a real part and an imaginary part. Picture one frequency as an arrow going round a circle. Let time run back into the page and its tip draws a corkscrew. Its shadow on the floor is a cosine (the real part), and its shadow on the back wall is a sine (the imaginary part):

$$e^{i\omega t}=\cos\omega t+i\sin\omega t.$$

Why half the FFT is a mirror

A real vibration moves along one line, but a single spinning arrow's tip also goes up and down. Fix that by adding a twin of the same size, spinning the other way. At every moment the twin is the first arrow's mirror image across the real axis.

  • When both point along the real axis, they sit on top of each other. Added tip to tail, they make one arrow twice as long: the peak of the wave.
  • A quarter turn later, one points straight up and the other straight down. Added, they cancel to zero.
  • In between, the up and down parts always cancel, so the sum only slides back and forth along the real axis. That is a cosine:
$$\cos\omega t=\tfrac12\,e^{\,i\omega t}+\tfrac12\,e^{-i\omega t}.$$

So every peak appears twice, at \(+f\) and \(-f\), each with half the size. For a real signal the second half of the FFT is the mirror of the first, \(X_{N-n}=\overline{X_n}\). Analysers drop it and double the rest: the peak you read is \(2|X_n|\).

The winding machine

Wrap the signal around a circle at a trial frequency \(f\) and find its centre of mass. Most of the time the wrapped signal balances out and the centre sits near the middle. When \(f\) matches a harmonic, it piles up on one side and the centre moves out. That centre is exactly what the Fourier transform computes:

$$X(f)=\frac{1}{T}\int_0^{T}x(t)\,e^{-i2\pi f t}\,dt,\qquad X_n=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x_k\,e^{-i2\pi nk/N}.$$

The second form, for \(N\) samples, is the DFT. The FFT is a fast way of computing it.

Real and imaginary, size and phase

For a signal written as \(x(t)=a_0+\sum_n\big[a_n\cos(n\omega_0t)+b_n\sin(n\omega_0t)\big]\), each bin is

$$X_n=\tfrac12\,(a_n-i\,b_n),\qquad 2|X_n|=\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}=c_n,\qquad \theta_n=\arg X_n.$$

The real part measures how much cosine, the imaginary part how much sine (with a minus sign). The length is the peak height; the angle is the phase in \(x(t)=\sum_n c_n\cos(n\omega_0 t+\theta_n)\). If you write harmonics as sines instead, \(c_n\sin(n\omega_0t+\varphi_n)\), the phase is shifted by 90°: \(\varphi_n=\theta_n+90^\circ\).

Work out one FFT bin yourself, from 8 samples and a calculator, in Lab lesson 4: An FFT by hand.

Phase is the shape

Keep every \(|X_n|\) fixed and change only the angles, and the waveform changes completely: square, spike, noise. The amplitude spectrum keeps the sizes and drops the timing, so it can't tell those signals apart.

Why analysts keep the phase

  • Balancing. The 1X phase, measured against a once-per-revolution reference, locates the heavy spot (allowing for the rotor's lag), so you know where to put the correction weight.
  • Across a coupling. Comparing 1X phase on both sides helps separate faults. A shift of about 180° across the coupling, especially in the axial direction, is a classic sign of misalignment. It's a guide, read together with amplitudes and directions. Why phase can tell imbalance from misalignment is a story for another post.
  • Resonance. As speed passes through a natural frequency, the phase shifts by up to 180°, passing 90° at the natural frequency, close to the amplitude peak. A large phase change with an amplitude peak is a strong sign of resonance.

Phase only means something relative to a reference: a tachometer or keyphasor for absolute phase, or a second sensor measured at the same time for relative phase.
